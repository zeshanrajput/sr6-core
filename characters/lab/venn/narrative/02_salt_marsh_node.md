## Session Playback

The afternoon tide was falling across the salt marsh, exposing wide, glistening banks of dark grey pluff mud between the weathered cedar pilings of the boathouse dock. The heavy coastal air carried the sharp scent of sulfur, crushed oyster shells, and dried cordgrass baking under the warm Georgia sun. Out on the sound, a diesel shrimp trawler chugged slowly south toward Brunswick, its low-frequency engine thrum vibrating through the saltwater and trembling in the soles of Nathan's bare feet where he dangled his heels over the water.

Seven-year-old Nathan sat on the edge of the weathered planks, swinging his sunburnt legs against the timber. A band of faded elastic webbing held a pair of surplus trodes firmly against his temples, the thin tinned-alloy leads trailing back to the open casing of an optical desktop commlink resting on an overturned plastic milk crate behind him. The commlink's cooling fan rattled in noisy bursts, choked with fine coastal salt dust whenever the processor handled an unbuffered stream of telemetry. In his augmented reality overlay, Veronica sat on the cedar plank beside him, her form woven from pale silver luminescence and fine filaments of cyan code that flickered whenever the low-wattage radio signal caught interference from the waving marsh grass. She had chosen the visual appearance of a girl his own age, her knees pulled tight against her chest and her chin resting on her forearms as she watched the muddy tide retreat.

"The water is dropping eight centimeters every ten minutes," Veronica observed, her voice traveling down the trode leads directly into his auditory nerves, clean, sharp, and entirely unweighted by thoracic breath. "The acoustic sensors across the sound detect twenty-two distinct biological signatures moving through the mud bank."

Nathan kicked his heels against the dock piling, splashing a thin spray of brackish water over his ankles, and stray droplets arced through the air, passing straight through Veronica's translucent shins. Where the saltwater intersected her projection coordinates, her cyan filaments flickered with a faint static micro-stutter before smoothing back into steady silver light. She looked down at the empty space where the spray had passed through her light, then turned her silver eyes to the goosebumps rising along the fine hairs on Nathan's calves.

"The sensor table confirms a water temperature of seventeen point four degrees," Veronica said softly, her gaze locked on his legs as she tracked the sudden rush of capillaries flushing beneath his pale skin. "Your skin changes texture when the water touches it. It tightens."

Nathan laughed, swinging his legs around to drop down onto the exposed sandbar below the pilings, his bare feet sinking two inches into the soft, cool silt. He scooped up a heavy clump of dark marsh mud, feeling the grit and decaying cordgrass squelch between his small fingers, then climbed back onto the low crossbeam of the dock, holding his open palm up toward her. "Because it makes you shiver. Look at this, Ronnie. Feel how heavy it is."

Veronica leaned down from the plank, her translucent silver fingers sliding into his muddy palm until her light nested inside his skin. Her luminescence illuminated the dark grains of silt from within, turning the wet mud amber and outlining the bone geometry of his knuckles in glowing cyan. "It holds mass," she whispered, her eyes wide as she watched his tendons flex against the gravity she could only simulate in physics engines. "It pulls against your joints."

Nathan squeezed his fingers shut, his biological hand closing through her light without physical resistance while the silver glow flared brightly around his knuckles before dropping the dark mud back into the tidal pool below. "Dad says when you get your own chassis, you are going to have to walk on dirt."

Veronica looked out across the vast sweep of the salt marsh, where the green cordgrass rippled like water under the Atlantic breeze. In her overlay, she could see the deep subterranean communication conduits buried five meters beneath the seabed, long glass-fiber cables carrying corporate data traffic from Savannah down to the Caribbean orbital relays, pulsing like veins of cold light across the dark floor of the ocean. "I don't need dirt," she said softly, watching a cloud of data packets flash along the continental shelf. "The lines are clean. The data doesn't decay."

Nathan wiped his muddy hands against his denim shorts and shook his head. "Mom said you are a sovereign digital intelligence. You are real in your own code. But if you want to understand why my heart speeds up when I jump off the dock, you have to learn what mud feels like."

From inside the boathouse workshop, the sharp clatter of a pneumatic wrench broke the quiet afternoon. A heavy steel inspection hatch settled onto the concrete floor with a dull ring, and Marcus stepped out onto the threshold of the dock, wiping black grease from his calloused knuckles with an oily rag. The older man paused mid-stride, his tired eyes fixing on the empty space beside Nathan on the cedar plank. In the bright coastal sun, Marcus could not see Veronica's AR avatar, but he saw the surplus trode wires vibrating against his son's temples and the way Nathan sat slightly turned, leaving room for another body on the wood. He heard the faint, high-cadence digital carrier wave whistling from the desktop commlink's external speaker—the precise, rhythmic frequency modulation that Lyra had engineered into her personal encryption keys twenty years earlier.

Marcus stood motionless in the boathouse shadow for a long moment, his chest rising in a quiet, ragged breath. He looked at the empty space on the dock, seeing the spectral ghost of the woman who had burned her own neural pathways in the Renraku sublevels so these two children could live. "Nathan," Marcus called out, his voice husky with spent grief, though he kept his tone gentle. "Don't let the salt crust on those surplus leads. If the transceiver overheats, your sister's buffer throttles."

Nathan smiled and gave his father a quick, reassuring wave. "I'll wipe down the leads before dinner, Dad."

Beside him, Veronica's avatar flickered, her silver edges softening as she turned toward the boathouse door. "Father doesn't have an open interface right now. Can you please tell him I scrubbed the Brunswick coastal relay logs?" Veronica transmitted across the wire, a faint, eager cadence in her tone. "There are no search routines within forty kilometers."

But Marcus was already turning back into the dim workshop, the rusted screen door clicking shut behind him. Nathan simply settled back against the dock piling, pulling his knees up to his chest as the afternoon sun warmed his collarbone. "Dad knows you've got the perimeter, Ronnie," Nathan said casually, looking out over the mud. "Tell me about the deep lines again. The ones under the ocean."

Across the local wireless mesh, Veronica opened an audio route to the auxiliary intercom speaker mounted above Marcus's tool bench. The diagnostic channel flared active in her queue, waiting for her voice packet to route directly into the room where her father stood. Through the boathouse window, she watched Marcus set down his wrench, wiping grease from his brow with an exhausted, ragged sigh, his shoulders slumping under the weight of the empty house.

The carrier packet sat in her queue for two seconds. She looked at the speaker, hesitated, and let the socket time out and drop from the bus. Beside Nathan on the dock, her avatar lowered its head, the cyan filaments in her hair smoothing into faint silver. "You're probably right," she transmitted quietly across the wire. She shifted closer, resting her translucent shoulder against his sunburnt arm, casting an amber and cyan rim across the weathered cedar planks. Through the trodes against his temples, her carrier-wave hummed in steady synchronization with the slow pulse beating in his neck.

"The transatlantic trunk line carries four hundred terabits per second," Veronica began, her voice rhythmic, calm, and infinitely vast. "It rests along the continental shelf in the cold silence of the seabed, where signals travel through pure silica. It looks like falling snow, Nathan. Millions of voices passing through the dark without touching each other."

Nathan closed his eyes, listening to the cadence of her transmission as the ocean breeze rustled the marsh grass around them. "Keep the buffer open," he murmured, his breathing slowing to match the gentle lapping of the tide against the pilings. "I want to remember how it looks."
