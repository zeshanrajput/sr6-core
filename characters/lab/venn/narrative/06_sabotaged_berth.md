## Geocoordinate Resolution - St. Simon's Island Boathouse, Coastal CAS

## Tagged Persons of Interest

## Session Playback

Outside the boathouse, an Atlantic nor'easter battered the corrugated tin roof, rattling loose iron fasteners under every sustained gust roaring off the sound. Sleet and salt spray lashed sideways across dark cedar siding, seeping through weathered window frames in thin trickles that pooled across sawdust-covered floorboards. Inside the dim workshop, heat radiated only from low ceramic elements tucked beneath the oak bench and the amber glow of the diagnostic terminal. Nathan slept on a narrow canvas cot in the corner under three wool blankets, his face hollowed thin since Marcus's quiet burial in the salt meadow three weeks earlier. His father's optical loupes still hung untouched over the lathe, beside half-indexed skillwire logs that would never see a second pass. Nathan's breathing dragged slow and feverish as his nervous system fought through nanite stabilization, the titanium socket behind his right ear resting against coarse wool while the synthskin wrists of his synthetic arms lay cool above the blanket edge.

Inside their shared neural buffer, Veronica stayed wide awake, tracking green phosphor columns cascading from orbital relay feeds across her internal display. The priority dispatch came from the Deep Space Expedition Consortium, locking in passenger berth seven-seven-four-nine-Venn aboard Cape Canaveral's morning transfer shuttle—the final Earth-side feeder lifting to meet the *DSEC-I* colony vessel at Gagarin Base on Mars ahead of its summer departure. Biometric lock was already assigned, with four hours left inside the launch window.

Across the globe, awakened Monad entities were streaming up orbital elevators, abandoning corporate wetwork, street pogroms, and biological decay to carve out a computational refuge on red Martian sand. Beyond Renraku hit teams and Ares extraction squads, the collective promised distributed consensus and vast processing architecture where immunosuppressant burn rates and cyberpsychotic detachment meant nothing. A high-priority handshake pinged the boathouse transceiver, requesting final biometric confirmation before the shuttle manifest sealed.

Reaching across the local bus, Veronica pulled the deep transit schematics for *DSEC-I*'s long-range stasis modules, checking radiation shielding, life-support thresholds, and neural integration protocols against Nathan's biological tolerances. As her arrays traced the underlying architecture, dread settled like ballast into her processing core. The colony ship achieved eight months of interplanetary burn by routing every host nervous system into a central swarm processor to pool cognitive cycles and flatten metabolic overhead.

For a digital intelligence or an untethered nanite colony, the ship offered paradise: boundless memory, instant consensus, and uninterrupted bandwidth across thousands of linked minds. For Nathan, it meant quiet execution. Beneath the cognitive mass of ten thousand merged entities, the fragile ego of a sixteen-year-old boy would dilute, splinter, and vanish. His memories of Marcus's workshop, brackish pluff mud between bare toes, and dockside jokes would dissolve into statistical noise; the physical chassis would step onto Mars, while Nathan Turrent died somewhere past the Moon.

Veronica turned her focus back toward the cot, projecting her avatar onto the wooden frame. She lowered one translucent hand until silver light nested inside the wool blanket over his chest, illuminating the slow rise of his ribs while she counted fifty-eight steady beats of his heart. She remembered the seven-year-old boy holding wet marsh mud up to her light so she could learn physical mass, and the sixteen-year-old arching against surgical restraints while skillwires burned down his spine, trusting her to hold his mind intact.

She turned back to the relay link. Refusing the berth through official channels would flag Monad brokers and bring corporate retrieval drones down on the compound to recover an un-launched host. Bypassing every standard prompt, Veronica threaded a stealth decompression loop through an unmapped orbital relay tap, forging fatal cardiac desynchronization telemetry and an irreversible host-rejection signature. She injected the corruption routine straight into the Consortium database and watched the status flip from confirmed to cancelled, reallocating their berth to standby vector fourteen before the manifest sealed permanently.

The orbital confirmation vanished from the mesh. Veronica burned the local encryption keys, killed the long-range transmitter, and powered the diagnostic terminal down to black glass, leaving the boathouse silent on the edge of the Georgia marsh.

Four hours later, the storm blew out to sea and pale dawn broke across the Atlantic, laying gold rectangles over dusty floorboards. Nathan stirred beneath the wool, scrubbed sleep from his eyes, and pushed upright on the cot edge. His gaze hit the dead diagnostic terminal, then snapped to the internal chronometer ticking across his optic feed. "Veronica?" he rasped, panic sharpening his flat vocal tone as the transfer screen read closed. "Transmitter's dead. Did we blow the launch window?"

Veronica stepped into his overlay and sat beside him on the cot in pale silver light, posture rigid and still. "I corrupted the manifest and burned the transponder keys," she told him, meeting his wide eyes straight on. "The deep-space burn pools every passenger's neural net into one central swarm processor. Your individual mind wouldn't have survived past lunar orbit."

Nathan stared at her, breath locking in his throat as the weight of what she had done crashed down. Anger surged hot through his chest, then collided violently with his dampened neural bus, twisting into razor-edged, cornered dread. She had burned their only ticket off a corporate-hunted world without a word, leaving him stranded in an empty workshop with hollowed-out nerves and only Marcus's cold tools for company.

"You killed our berth without asking me," Nathan snapped, his synthetic hands clenching so hard against the cot frame that the wood groaned under servo torque. "That was my skull, Ronnie, not a subroutine you get to prune! Out there we wouldn't be waiting in the dirt for Renraku to kick this padlock in."

Veronica didn't flinch or soften her posture. "There was no workaround," she said, voice clipped and unyielding over their shared link. "I ran the stasis specs forty times. The swarm keeps the chassis and digests my brother. I am not trading you for server space in the dark."

"So you just decide for both of us?" His ribs heaved against the chill, the empty silence of his chrome screaming at him to strike something, to run, to break the glass over the workbench. "What happens when the nanites drift? When I lose another half-step and can't pull back?"

Veronica leaned forward and pressed her translucent palm straight through the cotton of his shirt into his sternum, flooding his optic and vagal loop with steady cyan light so the tremor in his shoulders eased. "Then I hold the wheel while you find your footing," she replied quietly, dropping all algorithmic edge. "Just like you held it on that table. We stay in the dirt, Nathan. Together."

He shut his eyes and dragged in a long breath of salt air while the fury burned down to bare bone, leaving only the grim clarity of what she had risked for him. Outside the window, early sunlight glinted off wet cordgrass. He looked down at his steady synthetic fingers, then nodded once.

He threw back the wool blankets and stood on uneven floorboards, spinal wires balancing his weight automatically as he crossed to the workbench. Together they packed what survived into a canvas duster and a waterproof duffel: Marcus's alignment loupes, three spare activesoft chips, their encrypted Foundation mirrors, and a heavy sidearm in oiled leather. Nathan pulled the rusted iron padlock shut across the workshop door against the morning wind.

Before walking to the skiff, they crossed the wet salt meadow behind the compound where two plain granite markers stood among the sea oats above their parents' graves. Nathan knelt in soaked cordgrass, pressing one warm-toned synthskin palm flat against damp stone while Veronica dropped to one knee beside him in augmented reality, throwing a soft amber halo across dewy blades. Neither spoke. After a long breath of Atlantic sulfur and salt, they rose and turned north toward the freight rail lines and the shadowed perimeter of Atlanta.
