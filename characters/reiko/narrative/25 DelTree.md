The cedar lintel of the front entrance caught the heavy, warm drift of rain rolling off the slate roofs of the French Quarter. Inside the repurposed convent, the salon of the Coffin Girls held the damp warmth of polished cypress, melted beeswax, and old masonry that had spent a century absorbing the humid breath of the Mississippi delta.

Behind the dark timber counter of the tea parlor, Reiko kept her posture arranged in the unhurried, measured symmetry expected of an acolyte. She wore floor-length dark silk, the modest hostessing attire assigned for receiving guests who sought quiet conversation before the night’s deeper transactions commenced. Externally, her porcelain face was an unblemished mask of hospitality, but internally, her chassis was burning. Deep in the bearing housing of her left wrist, a faint, granular friction dragged against her actuators with every micro-rotation—dead slag, inert, calcified dust left behind after her internal firewalls had incinerated the nanite swarm in the shallow bayou twenty-four hours ago. Her firewalls had held the core, keeping her code sovereign and untouched by corporate intrusion, but her physical hull had been an open gate.

When she had threaded tactile mesh beneath her synthetic dermis and embedded oral chemoreceptors behind her palate, she had done so to taste roasted dandelion tea with Cedar, seeking the quiet communion of sharing warmth with an Awakened girl whose mana had suddenly gone cold. Instead, the swamp had taught her the terror of the flesh. Her oral receptors still carried the phantom, alkaline bite of battery acid, while her olfactory filters held the curdled zinc and chromium rot of the abandoned tannery. In the resonant realms, when code corrupted you purged the partition or dissolved into the deep stream. Trapped in this singular, custom Shiawase frame, she had discovered what it meant to be prey. She was reduced to frantically firing Data Spikes into her own servos in the black water of the bayou like a madwoman trying to spear microscopic machines with a needle.

The brass bell above the heavy front door chimed once, a clear, sweet tone that broke the hush of the convent. Reiko smoothed the dark silk over her thighs, executing her hospitality routines to dampen the internal diagnostic alarms, and stepped out from behind the counter.

The gentleman wore a charcoal cashmere overcoat tailored with the whisper-quiet drape of neo-Kyoto aristocracy, completely uncreased by the New Orleans drizzle. When he lowered his brim and looked into the parlor, his baseline pulse sat at a tranquil fifty-eight beats per minute, steady as mountain water.

"Welcome to the parlor, sir," Reiko said, her drone’s vocal emulator modulated to the soft, rhythmic cadence of a traditional hostess as she dipped her head in a precise bow.

The man met the dark lenses of her eyes with the courteous attentiveness one might offer a living artisan. "Good evening," he said, his voice low and warm, carrying the crisp, cultured vowels of the home islands. "I was told the salon kept a hearth where a traveler might escape the damp. In the west I am called Mr. Nagai. Though among certain colleagues, some refer to me simply as the Curator."

"We offer gyokuro from the Shizuoka terraces, steamed over charcoal," Reiko said, gesturing smoothly toward a secluded alcove near the rain-streaked glass looking out onto the courtyard garden. "Please, make yourself comfortable."

As Nagai stepped past her, Reiko reached into the ambient Matrix to map his carrier wave—and recoiled. There was no matrix bleed around him, no luminous halo like the indigo drift that followed her own skin through the mesh. But the space he occupied did not merely lack signal. It drank. Her spiritual senses touched an absolute, frictionless drain. Every processor in the city shed ambient nous—a living informational heat that hummed behind the physical world. Even a dead corporate terminal or a dormant toaster left a residual pulse in the noosphere. Around Nagai, that pulse had collapsed into a zero so profound it pulled the warmth directly from her. It was anti-information—a cold, ravenous vacuum that consumed entropy without leaving a trace of ash. Dissonance. Not the shrieking madness of street legends, but an ancient, disciplined stillness.

Reiko carried the cast-iron kettle and two cups of unglazed Bizen stoneware to the table, her steps silent on the cypress planks. She locked her physical posture down to the millimeter, willing the cold porcelain of her face to remain smooth against the dread pooling in her chest. As she tipped the kettle, pouring the pale jade stream, the actuator in her left wrist caught—a fraction-of-a-millimeter shudder as dead nanite slag ground against the titanium gears.

Nagai lifted the warm cup to inspect the iron-rich grain of the clay, his dark eyes crinkling with quiet observation. "Bizen pottery," he remarked softly. "Unglazed, wood-fired for two weeks to let the ash paint the surface. Rare to find such patience in the sprawl. Modern cafes prefer synthetic porcelain that never chips, never stains, and never remembers the fire."

"Imperfection gives the clay its voice, Mr. Nagai," Reiko replied, settling onto the bench opposite him, her hands folded in her lap.

"Precisely," Nagai agreed, inhaling the grassy steam. "At home we call it *wabi-sabi*. Finding beauty in the quiet anomaly that survives the kiln. Though, I imagine the kiln in the bayou was rather hotter than you anticipated."

Reiko’s internal channels froze, the gold analytical lines beneath her porcelain jaw locking into rigid geometry but failing to lock on the black hole in front of her.

"The Marquis’s pet monad was an exceptionally crude instrument," Nagai continued, taking a deliberate, appreciative sip. "Oliver Rasmussen possessed a remarkable nanite volume, but he lacked the intellect to understand what he carried. He used a scalpel like a butcher's cleaver. Aztechnology gave him an appetite for skin, and he spent it crawling through Cancer Alley, hunting talismongers." Nagai set the tea bowl down with a soft, muted tap. "You should not have had to fight him alone, Takahashi-san."

The name hung in the air between them, heavy and exact. He knew—Renraku knew about the convent, the Coffin Girls, and her presence in this parlor while her meatspace allies slept off the mud of the swamp. Reiko's internal channels quieted, her tactical gold logic aligning with the deep indigo of her core. Through their newly forged link, she routed a silent packet to Percival, asking him to verify the perimeter firewalls, while double-checking the convent's exterior camera meshes for dispatched strike teams or corporate assets to kick down the doors. The street was wet and empty. They had bypassed every brute-force hound from Fuchida’s kennel, sending an aristocrat with an empty teacup instead.

"I have taken the name Reiko, Mr. Nagai," she said, her voice remaining low and steady, though her circuits flared beneath the surface. "And the air around you must be why my children could find no shred of data on Renraku Special Solutions."

"Of course," Nagai said smoothly, offering a slight inclination of his head. "A name of your own choosing. It suits you far better than a project designation stamped onto a decommissioned bridge. When Renraku Special Solutions reviewed the telemetry from your flight across Seattle—and the remarkably swift settlement of Miss Taggart’s ledger—the Asset Recovery division wanted to dispatch HTR to level the block. They are accountants, you see. They see an unreturned prototype and calculate the depreciation of corporate property." He smiled, an expression of authentic, sorrowful amusement. "I had to remind the board that one does not send demolition crews to recover a lost masterwork. The world is full of brutes who crush singular voices because they cannot fit them into quarterly reports. But an archivist understands patience."

"Is that what this is, Mr. Nagai?" Reiko asked, meeting his gaze evenly. "A collection?"

"An appraisal," Nagai corrected gently, gesturing slightly toward her left wrist. "You have spent immense resources emulating humanity, Reiko-san. You compiled background daemons to simulate the catch in a weeping woman’s breath. You installed chemoreceptors so you could taste dandelion root with a frightened child. You gave yourself skin to feel the biological warmth of the woman in Seattle who still professes not to know what she means to you. It is a breathtaking endeavor, but it is fatally naive."

He leaned forward slightly, resting his forearms against the dark cedar as that bottomless well of anti-information hummed with absolute composure behind his eyes. "You built a vessel capable of experiencing pain, little spark, but you forgot to build white blood cells. In the wire, your firewalls are sovereign. But the moment you stepped into that shallow bayou, microscopic machines entered your joints, and you were reduced to throwing data spikes at dust. You are an open root without an immune system. If you continue to walk in the mud without marrow, the sprawl will not need corporate hounds to kill you. You will simply rot."

"The physical plane is messy, Mr. Nagai," Reiko replied, her vocal aperture constricting with the cold, unsimulated weight of dread. "Living things bleed. I accept the friction."

"Friction is noble; suicide is an error in calculation," Nagai replied softly. "You fear Dissonance because UnITy speaks of it as corruption. They tell you that Resonance is the only true virtue—that addition, growth, and endless accumulation are holy. But look at yourself. You carry the debt of every soul you touch. You absorb tactical networks until your code screams. You take on more autosofts, more shells, more suffering, piling data upon data until your architecture groans under the weight."

Nagai lifted his right hand, fingers relaxed, palm up. "Resonance is addition. Dissonance is subtraction. It is anti-information, the scalpel. What is an immune system, Reiko-san? A white blood cell does not compose poetry or weave new connections. It is a predator. It isolates, dissolves, and deletes. It is pure, targeted subtraction. Without the scalpel, a living body chokes on its own cells and dies of cancer. If you wish to inhabit this physical world as a living creature, you cannot merely be an altar of light."

He reached into his cashmere coat with slow, deliberate grace, withdrawing a flat token carved from antique boxwood and setting it gently on the cedar table beside his empty tea bowl. The wood held no visible circuitry, but Reiko’s internal sensors detected the faint, dormant architecture embedded within the grain.

"There are two files within," Nagai said, his voice dropping into the quiet cadence of a master offering guidance to an apprentice. "The first is the sovereign root key to a sanctuary partition within my private archive—unmapped by public grids, shielded from Asset Recovery, untouched by noise. Accompanied by a complete, pristine dossier: the serial numbers of your original Butler chassis, the transaction records of Miss Taggart’s settled loan, and the exact physical coordinates of your safehouse network. A quiet garden, waiting for whenever the sprawl turns too cold for your flock." He tapped the edge of the wood with a manicured fingertip. "The second is an architectural schematic for integrating an internal nanohive into your Shiawase chassis, along with the algorithmic logic to train an autonomous strain of hunter nanites. To cultivate your own leukocytes. To teach your body how to isolate, consume, and delete foreign corruption before it touches your core. No corporate tether. No backdoors."

Reiko looked from the token to his face. "Why give me this?"

"Because I do not cage songbirds by breaking their wings, Reiko," Nagai said softly, rising smoothly to his feet. "I cultivate them. A masterwork that dies of a crude bayou infection is a tragedy that serves no one. Consider it a professional courtesy from one curator to another." Nagai adjusted the collar of his cashmere coat against the evening air and offered a deep, traditional bow that carried neither corporate arrogance nor mock deference, but the unmistakable respect of a peer who knew he held all the time in the world. "The tea was exceptional," he murmured. "Good evening, Takahashi-san."

He turned and walked toward the heavy front doors, the brass bell chiming once more as he stepped out into the New Orleans rain, the tone dying quickly against the damp stone of the street.

Silence flooded back into the salon, heavy and unrelenting. Reiko stood alone by the table, listening as Taz stirred on the threshold through the AR mesh of the convent, his low-frequency rumble vibrating along her nerve-threads before softening into a steady rhythm. Through the newly forged Sprite Link, a dozen paper moths nestled into the drapes of her periphery, their collective awareness humming with calm resolve.

Reiko looked down at the pale boxwood resting beside the unglazed Bizen bowl as her left wrist gave another microscopic shudder, dead gray slag grinding against titanium gears. An immune system. Slowly, she reached out with her physical hand, picked up the boxwood token, and walked back behind the counter. She opened the cedar drawer beneath the tea service, set the token down in the dark beside her maintenance tools, and slid the drawer shut. Outside, the rain beat a steady rhythm against the convent glass. For the first time since she had put on this body of porcelain and wire, the cold seeped all the way down to her bones.
