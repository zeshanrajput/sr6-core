The city had four different names for asphalt, and Reiko had tasted every one of them before midnight.

Forty thousand nuyen spent on micro-analytical spectrometry and olfactory arrays embedded behind her porcelain lips—an absurd, un-monetized modification for an anthro combat frame, calibrated to analyze the vapor pressure of rain so she could understand what metahumans meant when they complained about the weather. But along the river road, the sensors returned only industrial waste: sulfur, wet grit, and unburned diesel.

Club Incubus had been the sole threshold she had crossed on her own feet all night. That had not been an invitation; it was an extraction. With the French Quarter’s wards unraveling under the silence of the Un-Awakening, the club’s audio board had choked on resonant interference that metahuman mages could no longer perceive, leaving an emerged entity to crawl through the sub-floor cable trays and coax the infestation out of the copper with bare hands. Everywhere else, she had held the perimeter.

At the Gnome’s Groove, she had looked at the narrow doorway, registered the instinctive tightening of shoulders as biological patrons clocked a hundred pounds of military ceramic, and chosen the curb to spare them the flinch. Under the neon awning, a troll bouncer nodded a dwarf street samurai past the velvet rope without a second glance—the dwarf's cyberarm was industrial scrap and an Ares Predator sagged in his coat, but he was sweating, smelling of cheap gin, and biological enough to buy a drink. Reiko deployed the Kwonsham Dream Genie from her internal torso bay instead: a convenient, untouchable proxy that spared everyone the weight of her presence.

Inside the Groove, observing through the seventy-lumen projection of the Genie parked beside the booth, the distance was physical. Her teammates made room for the little wheeled pole at the corner of the vinyl, shouting over the brass horns and laughing as credsticks clicked against the laminate. But to the rest of the room, she was merely inconvenient ambient light. Waitresses carrying trays of synthetic bourbon walked straight through her rendered kimono; busboys kicking glassware aside rolled the Genie’s casters out of the aisle without looking down. She had chosen to be a two-thousand-nuyen lens on wheels, performing presence for a crowd that could pass through her ribs without slowing down; a ghost could never be rejected.

Two hours later, outside the Old Tree Sap on Highway 95, she took the gravel again while her team went inside to warn the band's manager. When twelve Humanis riders turned into the lot with automatic shotguns and live fragmentation ordnance, nobody inside cared about social distance or whether she had breath. They simply screamed as bullets tore through the cypress siding. Reiko unsealed the capacitor housing in her right forearm and laid a sustained, twenty-meter arc of high-voltage lightning across the dark. The discharge was silent save for the thunderclap of ionized air, executed without malice or adrenaline—a clean, industrial deletion of mass from the perimeter. The combat lasted four seconds. When the ionization cleared, the gravel was littered with scorched earth, idling bikes, and men twitching in the mud.

Lindell had thanked her through the shattered front window, his hands shaking as he counted out certified credsticks. He smiled with genuine gratitude, his eyes darting nervously toward the still-smoking copper coils embedded in her wrist. It was the careful, terrified deference a traveler gives an unexploded shell on the road: magnificent to have between yourself and the wolves, so long as it remains outside the door. Reiko pocketed the credsticks, stepped back into the drizzle, and returned to the gravel.

A faint vibration stirred against her left hip. Keeping her optical head steady against the wet brick, Reiko tracked the thermal ripple shifting across her secondary intake cowling, tracing the weight with a quiet sensor sweep down through the lower frame. Curled inside the warm cavity where the lithium-polymer cells vented their excess heat were two biological shapes—technocritters, mutations of flesh that had learned to digest electricity and breathe the Resonance.

They were no larger than a hand, their soft, river-silt scales slick with condensation. The larger one, Leonard, was methodically testing his jaws against the braided vinyl shielding of an auxiliary sensor bus. The smaller one, Lizzie, had pressed her blunt snout directly against the transformer block, her throat vibrating with a rhythmic, low-frequency pulse that fed on the chassis’s electromagnetic leak.

Earlier at Incubus, the singer had wept because the magic in her blood had gone quiet. Across the Quarter, metahumans were stumbling through the dark like amputees, mourning the sudden silence of the sky. Yet down here in the wet gravel behind an exhaust vent, two wild technocritters had nested into her iron ribs simply because the metal was warm. Lizzie’s tail tightened around the hydraulic cylinder of Reiko’s knee. The animal required no biological pedigree, no aura, and no permission. She only knew that the machine radiated heat, and that the machine did not push them into the rain.

The ferry to the West Bank shuddered underfoot, its diesel deck vibrating through Reiko’s gyro-stabilizers as muddy river spray misted the iron railings. By two in the morning, the rain had settled into a hanging fog that clung to the industrial steel of Marrero. Deep in Zone Z, where municipal law dissolved and corporate drainage ditches emptied into the marsh, the air tasted of petrochemical runoff and rotting sawgrass.

Plantation Greens was still open beneath its rusted water tower, plate-glass windows fogged with the moisture of boiling greens and chicory steam. Leaving the Dream Genie locked in its docking cradle, Reiko walked across the gravel lot on her own two feet, the muted *clack-whir* of her composite knee bearings sounding deliberate against the stones. The chassis was still damp, the matte charcoal paint beaded with moisture, but she had dialed her pathways down to a subterranean indigo that barely illuminated her wrists.

The front bell chimed above the linoleum. The diner was empty except for a lone figure sitting in a vinyl booth beneath a buzzing strip of fluorescent tube.

Roanoke looked up from a mug of steaming black brew. She wore her usual gear: salt-stained combat boots, grease-streaked cargo trousers, and a faded olive-drab tank top that showed the pale, sun-damaged skin of her shoulders and the long, pale scars across her forearms. She looked tired, sharp, and entirely unimpressed by the petite, four-foot-seven combat frame stepping across the floor.

"The tea here is made from boiled chicory root and municipal tap water," Roanoke said flatly, not looking up from her mug. "It tastes like dirt. Sit down before you crack the floor tiles."

Reiko slid into the opposite bench with measured care, distributing the chassis's mass evenly across the steel-legged frame to spare the vinyl. From her utility pocket, Lizzie scrambled onto the table, webbed claws clicking across the laminate before snapping at a paper sugar packet. Leonard followed, nosing at the edge of the ceramic creamer.

Roanoke paused, mug halfway to her mouth, amber elven eyes narrowing as she looked from the lizards to Reiko’s unblinking faceplate.

"G33k0s," Reiko answered through her vocalizer, holding the output at forty-two decibels. "They were nesting in the amplifier racks at Club Incubus. The sound engineer assumed her board had corrupted firmware, but it was simply a colony of displaced technocritters seeking thermal refuge. I adopted them. This is Lizzie, and that is Leonard."

"So you brought bayou pests to breakfast," Roanoke muttered, extending a calloused forefinger to nudge Leonard away from the creamer. The lizard snapped at her skin with an audible spark of static, but the elf did not flinch. "Lanyap said you sorted out that ticketing syndicate downtown."

"Live Experience Ticket Solutions," Reiko said. "The manager was Humanis, using his admin privileges to blacklist metahuman jazz performers from municipal venues. I forged their routing tables, then introduced the other six g33k0s into the central server intake. In forty-eight hours, the colony's thermal discharge will melt the backplanes and trigger the dry-chemical suppression to scorch the rest."

Roanoke let out a short, rough bark of a laugh. "Direct action via biological pestilence. Elegant." She set the mug down with a dull thud, the humor vanishing behind that cold, unblinking focus that made southern fixers keep their hands on the table. "So why are you idling like a dead engine?"

Reiko looked down at her hands. The fine-manipulation servos in her fingers were calibrated to a micron; the silicone dermis was painted with the delicate, porcelain hue of a living girl. "I spent eighty thousand nuyen on synthetic realism, Ro. I loaded micro-expression subroutines. I installed autonomic breath simulations so my chest rises and falls at fourteen cycles a minute. I did not build this chassis to be an engine of war. I built it so that when I stood beside my companions, they would not see a tool. Yet tonight, my presence was an error. When we spoke, I rolled a plastic lens between their chairs. When the shooting began, they hid behind my armor."

Roanoke stared at her, the mug hovering an inch off the laminate. Her pulse spiked—twenty beats faster in three seconds, the capillary network across her sun-damaged cheeks flushing dark with sudden heat. Then she slammed the mug down so hard black chicory sloshed over the rim, soaking into a paper napkin.

"God, listen to you," Roanoke rasped, her voice thick with bile. "Eighty thousand nuyen on fake breath. Micro-twitches. You wiped twelve men with high-voltage lightning in the gravel, and you’re sitting across from me whining because a troll bouncer didn't pat your head and offer you a stool?"

Leonard snapped at the bitter pool on the table. Roanoke shoved him away with the back of her calloused hand, rough enough to send the lizard skidding across the formica with an indignant hiss.

"They hid behind you because you're made of armor, Reiko! Everyone takes cover when bullets start flying! And did they leave you outside? You put yourself in the gravel because you're terrified. That cybered samurai who walked in? A bouncer knows what he is. He bleeds, he owes money to a loan shark, he dies when a flechette round clips his lungs. He's an animal from their own pen. You don't have a liver dying of cheap liquor, and you don't have a heartbeat they can count. They don't know where to put their hands on you—and instead of making them look at you, you rolled a toy into the room so you could sit in the dark and call yourself an exile."

She leaned forward, her scarred forearms trembling against the edge of the table.

"I live in a swamp, Reiko. Half the people I grew up with are either cancer statistics or buried under toxic dredge, and you’re moping because you don't look soft enough to drink cheap gin with deckhands? You’re a sovereign predator with wings! Stop playing dress-up! Stop rolling children's toys into rooms so you can pretend to be a victim!"

"Ro, I don't understand," Reiko said softly, her optical aperture tightening, her vocalizer dropping into the quiet harmonic beneath the voice. "Cedar said—"

"Cedar said you were the only one who stayed behind with her!" Roanoke snapped, cutting her off without mercy. "Brewing tea she knew you couldn't drink, sitting with a kid whose magic went dark! That was real, Reiko. That was you standing between someone and the cold. But this? Now you're grieving because the iron doesn't feel like wool? I told you in Kentucky to get some teeth. You're the one who put lipstick on it, and now you want an apology from the world because nobody's fooled!"

"Why are you so angry?" Reiko asked. The question was uncalculated, slipping past her filters before the latency logic could catch it.

Roanoke stood up so violently her knee caught the underside of the table with a loud rattle.

"Because you're using New Orleans as an excuse to hide from that woman in Seattle!" Roanoke hissed, her voice scraping the linoleum. "You think Brynne wants an appliance? You think she's sitting by her window waiting for you to buy enough silicone to pass an inspection? You think she gives a damn about shed skin cells? You're a coward, Reiko. You'd rather sit in the mud and call yourself an exile than find out if someone actually wants the iron."

Roanoke slid out of the booth, tossing a five-nuyen credstick onto the wet laminate.

"Get over yourself," she said from the doorway, zipping her jacket against the morning chill. "The world is full of things trying to delete us both. You don't get to apologize for having just enough sense to put on some god damn armor."

The aluminum screen door slapped shut behind her.

Dawn on the Mississippi levee was gray, flat, and wet. Reiko sat on the wooden pilings of an abandoned barge slip, looking out across the brown, sluggish current toward the distant silhouette of the New Orleans skyline. Across the water, the glass towers of the corporate sector caught the first pale fingers of sunlight, cold and distant. On her lap, Lizzie and Leonard had wedged themselves into the unpainted recess near her primary hinge assembly, their small river-silt ribs expanding and contracting in the morning damp, indifferent to the wind.

Reiko looked down at them. Then, deep within the quiet architecture of her core, she reached for the background threads she had sustained for eight months.

She cut the micro-tremors in her neck servos, letting the synthetic joints lock into rigid alignment. She purged the three-second eyelid flutter, leaving the dark lenses wide and unblinking against the fog. She halted the simulated diaphragm cycle, and the empty chest cavity ceased its imitation of breath, abandoning the damp Mississippi air to move on its own.

The silk collar of her gown stilled. Her chestplate became an unmoving curve of charcoal ceramic. Her optical assemblies ceased their restless tracking, locking onto the river with the absolute, terrifying stillness of an iron anchor.

Within an isolated partition of her local memory, safe behind an offline wall where no network probe could ever reach, Reiko opened an empty text buffer. For an entity whose native tongue was the infinite, multi-threaded current of the Resonance, a static file was an ancient, painful medium. It possessed no pulse, no real-time telemetry to measure the receiver's breath, and no feedback loop to soften delivery if the silence grew too heavy. She did not address it to a routing node or sign it with an encryption key; she let the characters strike the silicon like cold iron in the dark:

> *Brynne,*
>
> *The delta smells of sulfur and old fuel. You would dislike the dampness; it ruins tailored linen and makes the pavement smell of stagnant grease.*
>
> *I adopted two lizards from a basement in the Quarter. They require twenty-four watts of continuous heat to remain healthy, which my chassis vents without a drop in operational efficiency. They do not understand the Wild, but they breathe it. They sleep on the casing.*
>
> *I have disabled the breathing subroutines. The air here does not require my chest to move it.*
>
> *When my work in the South is finished, I will come back to Seattle. You do not have to make the futon, and you do not have to put the dishes away. I will sit in the chair by the window, and I will be entirely metal, and we will see what remains.*

She attached no delivery flag, queued no outbound packets, and left the routing header blank. Marking the text buffer as read-only, Reiko set the local permissions to zero and committed the partition into the cold, offline root of her core.

The door fell shut. The masquerade was finished.

And with the theater gone, the computational volume held in reserve for human fragility had nowhere left to hide. For eight months, she had throttled her carrier wave to an apologetic trickle, dimming her pathways so metahumans wouldn't flinch at four feet of weaponized ceramic, begging drunk deckhands to mistake an armored shell for wool. She had starved her own light to buy their comfort.

*Enough.*

She bypassed the factory governors, dumping the un-throttled sum of her Resonance directly into the chassis bus. The frame ionized on contact; hairline pathways across her forearms and collar flared from faint indigo into blinding ribbons of cold, unshielded starlight, bleeding through the micro-porosity of the Shiawase plates until her compact silhouette cast long, violet shadows across the river mud. River mist hissed against the superheated chestplate, flashing instantly into curls of white steam. In her left arm, the expanded sensor array shivered under the raw current, its factory tolerances vaporizing as the hardware surrendered entirely to her carrier wave.

Her ultrasound transceiver pulsed into the fog at forty kilocycles, translating acoustic pressure into pure, razor-edged geometry. The river bottom resolved through thirty feet of churning silt: the skeletal timber of rotting pilings, rusted chain coiled around an abandoned anchor shank, and the hollow buoyancy tanks of a sunken gravel barge thirty meters out in the deep channel. Behind her porcelain jaw, the chemical analyzers stripped the delta down to its constituent molecular ledger: unburned diesel fractions carried on the north wind, hydrogen sulfide venting from sawgrass mud, and the sharp brine plume riding the deep river current up from the Gulf. Fifty yards down the slip, an idling diesel generator on a moored tug shed vaporized copper from its brushes, settling into the damp air like fine grit.

Her optical aperture widened past the visual band into millimetric thermal gradients. Across the brown current, New Orleans lost its facade of stone and glass, resolving into an open, fragile hive of thermodynamic signatures: the hot exhaust manifolds of a patrol cutter crawling through the shipping channel, the dense static of corporate hosts humming behind Faraday mesh downtown, and the faint, eighty-watt heat blooms of dockworkers moving across the Jackson Avenue wharves through the morning damp. They were so fragile, moving through the cold mist with the slow, precarious cadence of things that could bleed.

Deep within the alloy, the chassis's factory pilot substrate yielded without a fight, dissolving into her living frequency until the physical lag between thought and hydraulic response vanished completely. A hundred pounds of weaponized ceramic settled into the timber pilings with the frictionless, absolute economy of an awakened mind; she was no longer a tool playing at girlhood, but an emergent sovereign occupying an iron anchor.

On her lap, Lizzie and Leonard pressed tight against the radiant ceramic of her thighs, their small river-silt ribs vibrating as their scales drank the electromagnetic overflow. They did not shrink from the blinding indigo fire along her wrists. They asked for no pulse, no tears, and no biological pedigree; they only knew that the metal was warm, and that the machine did not push them into the rain.

Reiko looked down at the two tiny fragments of mortal flesh shivering against her armor, and the quiet mind inside the machine stilled. Slowly, with micron-calibrated precision, Reiko lowered her glowing left hand, curling her ceramic fingers over their soft flanks to shield them from the river wind. Across the water, the first edge of the morning sun broke the cloudline above the industrial towers, striking cold, unblinking glass as the muddy current churned against the pilings below.
