The fog did not roll off the Mississippi so much as it congealed, suspended between the brackish sawgrass of the drainage canals and the sulfur stench of the Marrero chemical plants. Beneath the rusted silhouette of the water tower, Plantation Greens held the heavy humidity of boiled collard greens. Damp linoleum gave way to the sharp, earthy bite of chicory root simmering on the back burner. Behind the laminate counter, Jose Madrigal was slicing overripe papayas for the morning smoothie prep, his radio murmuring static-choked zydeco from a pirate station across the river.

Reiko sat upright in the vinyl booth beneath the hum of a dying fluorescent tube, her posture held in the rigid, measured symmetry of the French Quarter parlor. The floor-length dark silk of her hostessing gown—delicate, high-collared, and tailored for tea service among the Coffin Girls—pooled against the cracked linoleum as an absurd, imported luxury. She had suppressed her surface pathways down to an apologetic indigo that barely grazed the porcelain finish of her wrists. Across the table, Roanoke had already finished half a mug of black brew. The elf wore her usual street kit: a faded dark green t-shirt, grease-stained cargo trousers, and salt-crusted combat boots that had kicked through every drainage ditch between here and Cancer Alley. Sun-damaged freckles mapped her pale shoulders, framing old briar scars and chemical burns along her forearms.

On the formica between them, two soft shapes nosed at a discarded paper sugar packet. Lizzie had wedged her blunt snout under the folded seam, tearing the paper while Leonard methodically circled the ceramic salt shaker. The two g33k0s Reiko had adopted kept their river-silt tails anchored against the warm exhaust cowling of Reiko’s lower chassis, drinking the steady twenty-four watts of electromagnetic runoff bleeding from her battery cells.

Roanoke set her mug down on a paper napkin with a dull thud. Amber eyes, bloodshot from three days in the marsh, locked onto Reiko’s unblinking optical faceplate.

"Jose’s coffee tastes like boiled swamp water," Roanoke said, her voice dry as parched reed grass. "Sit still before your gyro-bearings chew another hole in his benches. Lanyap said you settled that ticketing syndicate downtown."

"Live Experience Ticket Solutions," Reiko replied through her vocalizer, pinning her output to an even forty-two decibels to spare Jose's counter. She retuned her posture, servos carefully balancing her weight to minimize damage to the aged vinyl. "The manager was Humanis, using admin privileges to scrub metahuman performers from municipal contracts. I introduced six displaced g33k0s into the server intake. In forty-eight hours, the colony's thermal discharge will melt the backplanes and trip the dry-chemical fire suppression."

Roanoke grunted, extending a calloused knuckle to nudge Leonard back from the napkin. The lizard snapped at her skin with a harmless pop of static charge, but the elf did not flinch. "No firewall probing? No backdoor? Just biological sabotage? There's hope for you yet." The faint grin vanished, replaced by that cold, relentless focus that made street fixers keep both hands on the table. "So why are you idling like a junker spewing more filth into my air?"

Reiko folded her hands over the dark silk of her lap. "Because I think I'm making a mistake somewhere, Ro. I spent eighty thousand nuyen on synthetic realism for this chassis. I installed micro-expression subroutines. I calibrated an autonomic diaphragm so my chest rises and falls fourteen times a minute. None of this makes me better in a scrap. I did this so that when I walked beside the living, they don't see me as a tool. But no matter what I do, it's not working." Reiko raised her left hand, projecting a tight-beam ARO link across the table. "Take a look at my logs from last night. Let me show you what I mean."

A pale blue holoscreen opened in AR between their mugs, streaming timestamped sensor logs, LIDAR wireframes, and audio waveforms across the laminate.

"Look at the entry log for the Gnome’s Groove," Reiko said, her voice dropping into the quiet harmonic beneath her vocalizer. "The doorway was twenty-eight inches wide. When I reached the threshold, the patrons on the vinyl benches contracted their shoulder muscles by four percent. The troll bouncer shifted his stance, obviously noting my one hundred pounds of military ceramic and hardened poly-carbide. I didn't want to cause a scene at Lanyap's place, so I withdrew to the curb and deployed a Dream Genie from my torso bay. While my team laughed and drank, waitresses walked straight through me."

Roanoke's eyes hardened as a scowl crossed her features. She leaned forward, calloused fingers flicking through the ARO playback with ruthless speed, scrubbing the timestamp backwards frame by frame.

"Bulldrek," Roanoke said flatly. She paused the feed on the bouncer’s face, zooming in on the troll’s heavy brow. "Look at the timecode, Reiko. Twenty-three fifteen and four seconds. The bouncer looked down at you, paused for four-tenths of a second, and was opening his mouth, probably to ask for your cover. He didn't kick you to the curb. *You* flinched."

Reiko’s optical aperture constricted. Desperately, she pulled up vector lines and threat radiuses, letting the math crowd the air between them like a wall. "He was calculating the threat vector of a combat drone—"

"He was wondering if your little whorehouse getup had pockets for credsticks!" Roanoke snapped. She slid the vidfeed forward with a flick of her thumb. "Look here: a dwarf street samurai walked past him two seconds later with an industrial scrap cyberarm and an Ares Predator sagging through his coat. That dwarf probably smelled like cheap gin, grease, and bad debt. He took up space. He belonged in the mud because he didn't apologize for having metal in his skin. But you? The second someone looked at your plating, you panicked, backed into the gutter, and rolled a two-thousand-nuyen toy into the room so you wouldn't have to exist where people could see you. You did that yourself, Rei. Living down to the name, aren't you? You made yourself a ghost."

Roanoke swiped her hand through the projection, dragging up the next encrypted file. The wireframe resolved into the gravel lot of the Old Tree Sap on Highway 95, rain lashing against cypress siding beneath the flare of muzzle flashes.

"And this?" Roanoke demanded, tapping the red combat markers flashing across the log. "Twelve go-gangers. Automatic shotguns, flechette rounds, live fragmentation grenades. Are these audio spikes Lindell and the band screaming behind plywood walls?"

"I calculated the most efficient way to neutralize the threat," Reiko said softly. "A twenty-meter electrical discharge across a twenty-eight degree arc, forty kilocycle pulse. The engagement lasted four seconds. Twelve hostiles neutralized. When the ionization cleared, Lindell approached through the shattered glass to hand me certified credsticks. He smiled, but his heart rate sat at one hundred and twelve beats per minute. His eyes darted to the smoking copper induction coils in my wrist. He looked at me with the terrified deference travelers give an unexploded bomb. They hid behind my armor while the lead flew, and the moment the threat dissolved, they wanted the bomb back outside."

Roanoke stared at the screen, the temperature in her cheeks rising by four-tenths of a degree. She swiped the holoscreen away with a sharp sweep of her forearm, shattering the light into static.

"Twelve men with shotguns, Rei!" Roanoke rasped, her voice vibrating against the linoleum. "Twelve killers trying to turn that bar into a slaughterhouse! Lindell was not looking at an unexploded bomb; his hands were shaking because a grenade had just exploded outside the door! He was looking at the four-foot-seven ceramic angel who just saved his life! Of course they hid behind you! You're made of mil-spec alloy! Everyone ducks behind the armor when the shooting starts! That's what armor is *for*!"

Roanoke slammed her palm onto the laminate. Leonard hissed, skittering sideways to seek refuge beneath Reiko’s sleeve, while Lizzie flared her throat scales.

"Lindell handed you credsticks because you earned them," Roanoke snarled. "And what did you do? You took the scrip, bowed like a polite appliance, and walked straight back into the rain because you'd rather wrap yourself in loneliness than let a frightened man say thank you. You want everyone to treat you like a china doll, but the moment you have to do the work of living with people, you run."

Reiko sat motionless, her internal pathways flaring in an erratic indigo flutter behind the porcelain mask. The holoscreen wavered, static fraying the edges of the logs as the telemetry collapsed into dark air. "Ro, that's not fair. I stayed behind with Cedar when her magic went offline. I brewed roasted dandelion tea for three hours because she was terrified. I burned out my actuators in the bayou trying to rescue her—"

"Cedar loves you because you showed up!" Roanoke cut in, her voice raw. "You sat on the floor with a girl whose connection to the sky went dark, and you didn't ask her to justify her tears. And when we were kidnapped and unconscious in that slaughterhouse, you threw your own chassis over our ribs and took the shrapnel yourself. That was real, Reiko. That was you choosing to bleed for your friends."

Roanoke leaned across the table. Her calloused forefinger reached out and jabbed the center of Reiko’s chest—pressing through the warm, yielding synthetic dermis until she struck the firm composite frame beneath.

"Listen to that," Roanoke mocked, tapping the hidden structure again. "Hear that hollow hum? That's an empty ventilation fan pulling bayou fog into a titanium cavity fourteen times a minute. Who is that theater for, Rei? Me? The bouncer? Jose making smoothies?" Roanoke's eyes narrowed to amber slits. "Or that fixer in Seattle you're terrified to face?"

Reiko’s audio processing dropped two frames of latency. The ambient roar of the refrigerator compressors in the kitchen seemed to swell, filling the narrow booth with heavy, suffocating weight.

"I live in an industrial sump, Reiko," Roanoke said, her voice dropping to a harsh, resonant rasp that stirred the air above the chicory. "Half the families in Marrero are cancer statistics or buried under toxic dredge. The spirits I bargain with don't wear perfume. They don't pretend to be clean river nymphs singing in a mountain brook. They are made of sulfur, rust, and chemical sludge. They own their rot, and they own their power. They survive on the fringe because they don't apologize for their stench or their corrosive teeth."

Roanoke leaned in until Reiko could smell the bitter chicory and woodsmoke on her breath.

"And you?" Roanoke whispered. "You have wings in universes we can't even dream of. You survived corporate hounds, you pulled down gunships in Seattle, and you saved my life. And you’re sitting across from me whining because a troll bouncer didn't offer you a stool? You spent eighty thousand nuyen on synthetic lungs and micro-twitches just to beg crumbs of pity from drunk deckhands? Frak that noise. You're drekking on every outcast spirit and broken soul clawing for breath in this delta."

An internal actuator alarm flashed in Reiko's peripheral feed: her ceramic fingers were biting two millimeters into the laminate edge of the table.

"I told you in Kentucky to get some armor so you wouldn't die in the dirt," Roanoke pressed, her breath hot against the porcelain jaw. "I didn't tell you to paint lipstick on it and apologize for having teeth."

"Ro—"

"You're hiding," Roanoke spat. "You're using New Orleans as an escape so you don't have to go back to Pulaski Street. You think Brynne wants an appliance? You think she survived months in a shipping container waiting for you to buy enough silicone to pass an inspection? You think she cares about simulated skin cells? You're a coward, Rei. You'd rather sit in the bayou mud and call yourself an exile than find out if someone actually cares about who you are, not what you are. News flash: I didn't check if you had a pulse back when you were walking around butt-naked in a god damn Nissan Samurai."

Roanoke stood up. The steel legs of the booth groaned against the linoleum. From her cargo pocket, she pulled a worn certified credstick and tossed it onto the wet laminate. It landed beside the sugar packets with a sharp clatter.

"Get over yourself," Roanoke said, turning toward the door. "The world is full of things trying to delete us both."

The aluminum screen door swung wide. A gust of wet, sulfur-tinged morning air swept through the diner, carrying the rumble of a diesel barge on the river. The screen door slapped shut against the frame.

Silence settled over Plantation Greens.

Reiko remained in the booth. On the table, Leonard crept out from her sleeve, nudging the edge of the credstick before curling into the warm hollow between her wrists.

---

Dawn over the Mississippi levee broke without color, a flat pewter gray blurring the boundary between river and sky.

Reiko sat on the rotted timber pilings of an abandoned barge slip, looking out across the sluggish, brown expanse of the shipping channel. Across the water, the glass towers of downtown New Orleans stood like distant headstones, cold and pale behind the industrial haze. Muddy current slapped rhythmically against the creosote pilings below, carrying sawgrass debris and oily rainbow slicks toward the Gulf.

On her lap, Lizzie and Leonard had wedged themselves into the recess near her hip hinge. Their soft, river-silt flanks expanded and contracted against her composite plating, completely indifferent to the damp wind blowing off the marsh.

Reiko looked down at her hands. The fine-manipulation servos in her fingers rested motionless on the wet timber. Beneath the synthetic dermis, her internal diagnostics hummed with uninterrupted, mechanical precision.

Deep within the architecture of her core, she addressed the emulation layers she had sustained for eight months. She terminated the cervical micro-tremor routines; the simulated tension dissolved, and her synthetic neck bearings dropped into rigid, locked alignment. She purged the eyelid flutter loop, leaving the dark lenses wide, drinking the morning gray without blinking. Finally, she reached for the autonomic daemon in her chest. Fourteen cycles a minute, drawing humid bayou air across inert heatsinks just to make the silk of her gown rise and fall. She sent the instruction down the bus: an immediate, unconditional halt. The miniature intake baffle clicked shut. The empty carbon cavity ceased its imitation of life. Her chestplate settled into an unbroken, unmoving curve of military ceramic, abandoning the wet delta wind to move on its own.

Within an isolated partition of her local storage, walled off from Matrix carriers and corporate sniffers, Reiko opened an empty buffer. She addressed it to no routing node, attached no delivery flag, and queued no outbound packets. She simply let the characters strike the cold silicon:

> *Brynne,*
>
> *The delta smells of sulfur and old fuel. You would dislike the dampness; it ruins tailored linen and makes the pavement smell of stagnant grease.*
>
> *I adopted two lizards from a basement in the Quarter. They require twenty-four watts of continuous heat to remain healthy, which my chassis vents without a drop in operational efficiency. They know nothing of the Wild; they simply breathe it. They sleep on the casing.*
>
> *I have disabled the breathing subroutines. The air here does not require my chest to move it.*
>
> *When my work in the South is finished, I will come back to Seattle. You do not have to make the futon, and you do not have to put the dishes away. I will sit in the chair by the window, and I will be entirely metal, and we will see what remains.*

She locked the file to read-only, committed the partition to cold offline storage, and let the thread go silent.

The river wind gusted across the slip, spraying fine mist against her cheek. On her lap, Lizzie shifted, pressing closer to the unmoving warmth of the battery cowl, while Leonard’s throat pulsed in time with the low, electrical hum of her frame. They did not search for a pulse. They did not ask for tears, a biological pedigree, or the rise and fall of a ribcage. They only knew that the machine was warm, and that she did not push them into the rain.
