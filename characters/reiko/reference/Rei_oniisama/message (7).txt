“Please state your name and the part you’ll be auditioning for today.”

“James Williams.  I’m auditioning for the role of Franz Liebkind in The Producers.”

“What song will you be singing for us today?”

“The Impossible Dream, from Man of La Mancha.”

“Proceed.”

James launched into the song with over the top sincerity.  The kind of earnest absurdity that Franz himself would’ve approved of.

“Thank you.  And what monologue will you be performing today?”

“Alceste’s monologue from Act 1, Scene 1 of The Misanthrope.”

“Proceed.”

James delivered the speech exactly as he imagined Franz would: dramatic, with just a touch of unhinged.

After the audition concluded, James thanked the panel for their time and consideration.  He knew he’d run into them later that night at The Green Room Collective, where he’d be expected to win them over with flattery and rounds of drinks.  All part of life at the Capitol Hill Community Playhouse.

The night went well.  One of the panel members, Janet, offered to personally coach James for the role of Franz.  She noted that daytime sessions — when her husband was at work — would be “best.”  James smiled, accepted the offer, and politely insisted he wouldn’t want to impose until casting was finalized.  Janet traced a finger down his chest, smiled back, and assured him the role was already his.

Casting was announced, and James landed the part of Franz Liebkind.  Janet was true to her word and expected the same from James. There was more couch than coach in their sessions, and the real rehearsal came under the watchful tutelage of Fox.  The next eight weeks flew by as Rei balanced practice with Fox, Playhouse rehearsals, and his actual work as a shadowrunner.  He learned more about acting, makeup, and performance than he ever expected, and by opening night he felt confident that James was ready.

Opening night arrived, and James was nervous.  Though he claimed to be an accomplished actor, this was his first time on a real stage, and Franz’s entrance set the comedic tone for the entire show.  But once the house lights dimmed and the music started, James’ nerves melted away as he slid into Franz.

The next day’s review in the local paper praised the production, noting that the Capitol Hill Community Playhouse delivered “in only the way they could.”  The troupe was thrilled by the unexpected turnout, though they couldn’t help noticing that some of the audience looked a little rougher around the edges than their usual patrons.  The kind of folks who carried more chrome than cred.  Still, an audience was an audience, and more filled seats meant they were climbing the ladder of Seattle’s arts scene.

After the final show, James once again found it necessary to leave Seattle for a while.  Rumor had it that Janet’s husband had come home unexpectedly and discovered the two of them “celebrating” the show’s conclusion.  According to those in the know, James didn’t even have time to fully dress before bolting.  He barely managed to swing by The Green Room to say goodbye to the troupe.

“You didn’t do too bad as an actor, Rei.  You could always ditch the shadows and take up performing.”

Rei snorted.  “Thanks, Fox, but I’m good.  I prefer my life just the way it is.  Which reminds me, did you have to tell everyone I work with that I was doing community theater?”

Fox laughed.  “Tell them?  Chummer, that news spread through the Seattle shadows like wildfire.”

Rei sighed.  He could already imagine the ribbing waiting for him.

Fox smiled, and asked “So…did you get to keep the lederhosen?”
