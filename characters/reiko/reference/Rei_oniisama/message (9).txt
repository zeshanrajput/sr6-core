Rei excelled in close combat.  Especially blades.  An adept whose magic sharpened every strike and parry.  The trouble started whenever the drek hit the fan at range.  Shooting wasn’t the issue.  Half the runners he knew joked he was a gun adept pretending to be a sword saint.
No, the problem was not getting shot.
Lately he’d been spending more time with Sato to fix that little issue.  Rei knew he leaned too hard on his natural (and supernatural) talents.  Sato knew it too, and he was determined to beat the habit out of him.
Over several weeks, Sato drilled him in the subtle art of gymnastics and tumbling.  Rei trained on the parallel bars, the pommel horse, and the rings.  And because it wouldn’t be Sato style training without pain, the old man kept a cattle prod handy to remind Rei he wasn’t prepping for the fraggin’ Olympics.
And as always, there was a test.
Rei waited in the clearing outside Sato’s house when he heard the faint whir of an electric motor.  He braced himself and still wasn’t ready for what rolled into view.
Sato, in a motorized wheelchair, wearing a WWII bomber jacket and a stained white silk scarf like he’d stepped out of some ancient trid.
“Sensei, what happened?  Are you okay?”
Sato ignored him completely.
“If you’re going to learn to be a real dodgeballer, you’ve got to master the five Ds,” he said. “Dodge, duck, dip, dive, and dodge. Learn those, and no amount of balls on Earth can hit you.”
Rei blinked.  Definitely a movie reference.  No idea which one.
“So… I’m dodging balls today?”
“No. That’s why I brought this sack of wrenches.” Sato hefted a canvas bag full of metal. “If you can dodge a wrench, you can dodge a ball.”
“What?”
A wrench whistled past his ear before he finished the word.
The next hour was a master class in why you don’t get hit by a wrench.  Weeks of training paid off… mostly.  A few grazes, a couple solid hits, nothing catastrophic.  Sato barked pointers the whole time, drilling focus, movement, awareness.
As the supply of throwable objects dwindled, Rei actually felt good about the session.  Sato let him catch his breath, and they talked about reading an opponent’s aim—because no one outruns a bullet.
That’s when Rei heard the unmistakable sound of an Ares Predator VI chambering a round.
The next hour built on everything before it.  Sato “only” used gel rounds, but anyone who’d ever taken one knew they left a memory.  The lesson was simple and painful.  Don’t be where the enemy is shooting.
By the time the sun dipped low and they headed inside, Rei finally asked, “Okay, seriously—what’s with the getup? I know it’s from a movie, but… really?”
Sato laughed. “Dodgeball:  A True Underdog Story.  Excellent comedy.  Grab a shower.  We’ll watch it after dinner.”
