# Heat

The oily smoke of the incinerator barrel thinned into the North Sea drizzle, leaving only a crust of melted ballistic weave and charred resin at the bottom of the rusted drum. Across the canal, the yellow floodlights of the Europort terminal sliced through the fog, silhouetting the massive gantry cranes of Ruhrmetall Logistics as they stacked automated freight containers with rhythmic, hydraulic thuds. Velvet watched the last scrap of blood-spattered synthetic cotton burn to gray ash before stepping back into the shelter of the quay wall.

With a subvocal tap against the throat mic, Velvet cued Ai-chan. The commlink app, an enterprise Personal Assistant package hosted on the Erika Elite, instantly engaged its client-side graphics pipeline. It was no sentient digital mind, merely a ruthlessly efficient model parsing local telemetry, monitoring sensor feeds, and orchestrating tools across Velvet's personal area network. Ai-chan queried the ambient lighting parameters, calculated the refractive index of the falling rain, and projected a tailored navy cashmere trench over Velvet’s shoulders. To any dockworker or harbor drone scanning the wharf, the illusion was immaculate, complete with crisp lapels, realistic wool physics, and matte silver buttons catching the sodium glare. Beneath the holographic light, however, the damp Dutch gale bit straight into the bare SecureTech skinshield, slick with sulfur residue.

Even with a fire ancestor coaxed into the hearth of the ribs, the North Sea chill worked into the marrow. The ancestral spirit rested warm against the sternum, radiating dry, talismanic heat that kept the blood moving, though it could not stop sixty kilometers of maritime damp from clinging to exposed skin. Pushed forward by the harbor gale, Velvet crossed the narrow cobbles and stepped through the frosted glass door of *De Vries & Zoon*, an outfitter tucked between two freight forwarders along the canal quay. Radiator heat carried the alkaline bite of dry tailoring chalk and hot lanolin from the bolts overhead, heavy enough to push back the bitter chicory simmering in an enamel pot behind the counter. Rolls of double-faced Antwerp wool and oiled sea-canvas were stacked to the acoustic tile ceiling, deadening the low-frequency thrum of container tugs out in the basin.

Behind the basting table, an elderly clothier in a corduroy vest looked up, a yellowed wooden measuring tape resting across his stooped shoulders. The man wiped his steel shears with an oily rag, his pale eyes cataloging Velvet’s posture with the indifferent precision of an old port tradesman. "Closed in ten," the man said in Flemish-accented English, laying the shears on a cutting block without glancing toward the door. "Unless you take off the rack."

"Off the rack is fine," Velvet said, shifting smoothly into Lawrence Duval’s precise, continental French cadence even as a dull ache flared through the base of the skull while his larynx settled into the new register. "Charcoal overcoat, heavy knit underneath—something that moves quietly through customs offices."

The old clothier stepped from behind the laminate counter and reached out to gauge the shoulder seam of Duval’s navy trench. His dry, calloused fingers passed straight through the holographic projection, striking the cold polymer of the skinshield beneath. Pulling his hand back smoothly, he squinted through the blue wireframe of the AR garment at the bare torso and the red telemetry markers Ai-chan was flashing along the dermal seams. "The light show is very modern, *meneer*," he murmured, his tone as dry as pumice. "Very sharp for a video meet in Brussels, though light does not stop fifty kilometers of Maas wind."

"No," Duval said, sending a silent subvocal impulse to Ai-chan. The navy blazer dissolved into the yellow bulb-light of the shop, leaving only the neutral, charcoal-grey line of the skinshield against the damp pine floorboards. "It does not."

The outfitter measured the scorch marks along the dermal sleeve with a single indifferent flick of his thumb before turning to the wall rack. In a port city where cargo went missing between Ghent and Europort every night, an unclad customer with certified creds was merely another Tuesday transaction. He pulled a bolt of double-faced Belgian wool from the shelf, unrolling three yards across the basting board with a muffled thud before nodding toward a low oak pedestal in front of a three-panel dressing mirror. "Stand on the round," he grunted, hooking his thumbs behind the yellowed measuring tape.

Velvet stepped onto the wood. As the cold brass tips of the tape brushed collarbone and spine, he shifted his features by fractions. Beneath living skin, biological levers answered conscious will: the jawline squared to support Duval’s crisp Gallic vowels, the clavicle broadened to hold the drape of European cloth, and the ribcage compressed to steady the lungs. The internal bone-latch clicked behind the ear, metabolic heat swallowed without a flinch.

In the three-panel mirror, the previous forty-eight hours flickered against the glass. In Ghent, two automated freight trucks loaded with smiling green dragon plushies had been paid for when the rest of the crew's ambush shattered a Saeder-Krupp Johnson's guard detail under machine-gun fire. Yet forty minutes before shooting tore that loading dock apart, beside a muddy canal pitch, Velvet had watched hardened syndics drop their weapons to shout for their children in the downpour. The memory settled behind his newly sculpted eyes, anchoring the stubborn endurance of ordinary people surviving beneath loud guns and corporate titans.

The clothier tossed a charcoal overcoat over his customer's shoulders. Ten pounds of dense, boiled wool pressed the fire ancestor's warmth back into the chest, raw fiber scratching abrasive and real against the neck. Pinning the left cuff with three swift jabs of his needle, the old man smoothed the back seam with his palm. "Good wool has memory," he muttered, stepping back to check the fall of the shoulder. "You treat it right, it holds the shape of the man wearing it long after the weather breaks."

"Let us hope so," Velvet replied in Duval's easy baritone, sliding his arms through the silk lining and stepping down from the pedestal. "Monsieur, if I may ask—word around the quay is that the dock families were stirred up after what happened in Ghent. I had hoped to make their acquaintance before my transport departs."

The outfitter paused, smoothing the chalk line along the lapel before looking up. "A messy business near the rail yards," he warned, keeping his voice low beneath the radiator hiss. "S-K security drones have been buzzing the canal since dawn. Best not to ask questions near the cranes, and best not to be seen."

"I agree completely," Duval replied with a polite inclination of the head, resting one gloved hand on the wooden counter edge. "And yet, a man in my line of trade cannot leave the Maas without speaking to the people who control the water."

The old man tapped the end of his wooden tape against his palm, weighing the cut of the wool against the French accent. "Walk four blocks past the lock gate. There is a bathhouse behind the eel smoker with a red tile roof and no Matrix sign. The dock stewards go there to sweat out the cold and wash the diesel from their hair. Tell the girl at the counter that Pieter sent you for the birch steam. If anyone is willing to talk business while S-K sweeps the harbor, you will find them there."

Velvet pulled three certified credsticks from the commlink pouch, laid them on the basting table with extra for discretion, and buttoned the dense wool coat to the throat. Stepping out into the sulfuric Europort fog, the North Sea wind broke harmlessly against the boiled Belgian weave, leaving the vessel inside dry, warm, and opaque.
