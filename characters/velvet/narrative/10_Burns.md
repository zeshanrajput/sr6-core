# Burns

The copper wash-vats in the basement beneath the Sham Shui Po apothecary simmered over low gas rings, filling the stone cellar with pungent white vinegar and crushed camphor leaves. Above the low rafters, morning industrial laundry clattered, sending fine plaster dust drifting across unbleached cotton gauze and rusted zinc tubs. Velvet sat on an overturned tea crate in the corner, elbows on his knees, breathing in the sour heat to scour scorched insulation from the back of his throat.

Stripping away two distinct lives in six hours had extracted a steep biological tax. The elder dignity of *Zhang Wei* had died first, shattered in the cordite haze and splintered shelving of the Jade Crane storage facility when the Red Dragon enforcers drew down. Then, on the rain-slicked concrete of Kwai Chung lot CT4, *Leung Hoi-ching’s* working-class harbor existence had been burned away beneath a summoned gale. When the air spirit’s lightning arced across the container stacks, strobing the berths in violet-white, fleeing syndicate soldiers caught Hoi-ching’s face under the floodlights just as CorpSec patrol sirens started their wail.

Velvet reached up with bare, trembling fingers, unfastening the stained high-visibility vest and grease-marked linen shirt of the harbor clerk, and dropped both into the iron drain at his feet before releasing the active spell-work. The cosmetic matrix dissolved from his skin with a dry, searing heat. Deep within the zygomatics, compressed bone-latches clicked heavily through the skull as his geometry widened. The broadened mandibular frame and shortened clavicles of Hoi-ching eased back into their natural alignment, spinal vertebrae settling with a dull, grinding throb that radiated down into his lower back. He closed his eyes against the sharp migraine behind his temples, waiting as dark obsidian pigment flooded back into the outer rings of his irises, washing away the false amber of the docks until only the raw, un-sculpted baseline of Kim Jin-Young remained.

On the rough pine table beside the wash-vat, three certified credsticks lay beside an empty brass burner. The ledger was an operational disaster: Mr. Fa’s panicked transfer had cleared at seven thousand nuyen, and the shadows in Hong Kong demanded their toll in blood and scrip. Ten percent had vanished into the coat pocket of a Port Authority watch commander at the outer gate—crossroad coin pressed into a nervous palm to ensure the security subroutines logging their vehicle were purged before CorpSec arrived. Twice that sum had gone directly to the elderly herbalist upstairs for clean saline, sulfur burn pastes, sterile shears, and dry woolen blankets, leaving the contract at a net financial loss once transit chits and ferry bribes cleared.

Mitsuhama’s actuarial routines, hardwired into his early education, logged the deficit as failed resource management. Yet looking at the red balances on his commlink slate, his pulse stayed slow and steady—nuyen was spent tallow, burned to keep the wolves off the threshold.

Across the stone floor, huddled against the hot-water pipes, sat seven street kids and runaways pulled from shipping container CT4. Wrapped in coarse grey wool, their small frames shivered with lingering static aftershocks from the dockland assault. The storm had breached the perimeter, yet an air spirit possessed no concept of thermal conductivity; when lightning struck the corrugated alloy of the holding pens, current surged through the structural ribs, superheating the interior metal and showering the captives in molten slag. Near the boiler, a fifteen-year-old girl hissed in pain, clutching a forearm where charred cotton had fused directly into blistered skin.

Velvet stood up from the crate. His legs were stiff from the cartilage reset, and the tremors in his hands were a lingering reflex of corporate adrenaline that needed to be cleared. In Mitsuhama’s idol facilities, coaxing the spirits had been drilled as a cold, biomechanical protocol: align the mental vectors, force the mana through neural pathways like high-voltage current, and bind the external entity as a disposable battery to bear the feedback.

He stepped to the center of the flagstones, lowered his chin, and emptied the vessel. He cleared the floor of his own ego, relaxing the tension in his diaphragm and inviting the spirits across the threshold.

The air in the cellar shifted. The caustic tang of vinegar and zinc softened, replaced by an ancient warmth carrying the dry scent of wild cedar drying over an autumn hearth. For once there was no icy, burning back-voltage of corporate spellcraft; it settled across his collarbones with the comforting weight of a heavy wool blanket. His posture shifted reflexively, breathing deepening as an unhurried, ancestral stillness anchored his frame.

The spiritual touch moved across his skin in a soft, measured invitation. Velvet released his hold on his cosmetic spell-work, letting the presence take root. Beneath the surface, metahuman geometry yielded: the tapered cartilage of his ears rounded into human lobes, while the obsidian of his Dalakitnon irises warmed into everyday tea-brown. Her ribcage drew inward into a gentle curve, settling into an ordinary woman's frame as her skin took on the dry warmth of unglazed earthenware and the calculating lines of his face tapered into a village herbalist.

Velvet knelt on the cold stone before the injured girl. When her hands reached out, the tremors were gone. Her fingers moved with an ancient, rhythmic economy she had never studied in corporate training. With a pair of dull brass shears, she cut away the blackened sleeve, her touch so light and steady that the girl’s sharp gasps slowed to quiet whimpers. She dipped a clean linen cloth into a basin of cold herbal tea and boiled saline, cleansing the soot and metal flakes from the raw tissue.

The spirit investing her turned her unearthly presence inward, dampening the active glare of her aura until it became a deep, sedative pool. Gently, the room’s ambient anxiety unwound. Across the flagstones, the other children stopped their restless shifting, their rapid heart rates slowing in response to the quiet, immovable peace radiating from the kneeling figure.

With methodical precision, Velvet applied thick coats of cooling camphor salve across the burns, smoothing strips of unbleached cotton over the wounds. When she tied the finishing knots at the wrist, her fingers moved in a distinct, looping braid. It was an old method of untying tension and binding trauma that owed nothing to modern plaster casts.

She moved to the next child, an underfed boy nursing a dislocated shoulder suffered when the container doors were blown inward. Velvet placed one warm palm flat against the boy’s sternum, letting the ancestral weight transfer through the touch to ground the child’s panic. With a single, fluid traction of her other hand against the wrist, the joint slipped back into its socket with a soft, clean pop. The boy flinched, blinked through tears, and then let his head fall forward against Velvet’s shoulder, exhausted beyond fear.

In the corner of the ceiling, a grease-stained trid monitor flickered to life, audio turned low above the boiler. The Hong Kong news feed displayed aerial footage of the Kwai Chung container terminal. Emergency strobes from Port Authority cruisers painted the gantry cranes blue, while heavy corporate drones hovered over the twisted wreckage of lot CT4. A polished anchor praised local authorities for breaking up a syndicate human trafficking ring, cutting briefly to an interview authored by Fa Ren, the journalism student whose university thesis had broken the story wide open. On screen, Fa Ren stood outside her father’s apartment, exhausted but resolute, speaking of accountability and corporate negligence with fierce clarity.

Velvet kept her hands steady on the gauze, tied the final bandage, smoothed the wool blanket over the boy’s shoulders, and stepped back. The ancestral presence receded like a warming tide, leaving her muscles sore yet her center clear. All seven children slept deeply against the hiss of the copper vats.

She climbed the narrow wooden stairs to the street, pulled on a plain windbreaker from the apothecary’s drying rack, and zipped it to her throat. She had no active face in this sprawl, no corporate identification, and barely enough nuyen to buy passage on the morning ferry.

At the final landing, a full-length mirror leaned against the wall. A delicate, soft-featured young woman looked back, short dark hair plastered to her forehead by sweat and steam. Reaching up to touch her cheek, she asked the glass, "What is your name?" Her mouth moved of its own accord, shaping three unfamiliar syllables: Kang Anning.

Pushing open the heavy alley door, Velvet stepped out into the damp, sulfur-tinted dawn of Kowloon. Across Victoria Harbour, the emerald spire of the Wuxing Skytower caught the first pale light of morning, cold and untouchable above the mist. Velvet pulled her hood low against the harbor drizzle, turned her back on the tower, and walked down into the crowded streets with clean hands, an empty ledger, and an unburdened soul.
