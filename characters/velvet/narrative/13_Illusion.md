# Illusion

The tide came in beneath the floorboards in a slow, three-beat cradle: the long draw of the North Sea over wet sand, the soft crest against timber pilings, and the quiet sigh of foam receding into the dark. Inside the winter pavilion at the end of the Scheveningen pier, the blizzard that had buried The Hague forty-eight hours earlier was only a crust of salt along the lower windowpanes. Cast-iron radiators hummed low beneath the wainscoting, warming the air with beeswax, dried sea-lavender, and the pot of chamomile steeping between two porcelain cups. Beyond the curved glass, the clouds had parted over the water, letting a pale wash of moonlight silver the rhythm of the swells.

Velvet sat at the corner table in the quiet geometry of Alix Lindqvist, turning a small square of heavy, unwatermarked cream linen between gloved fingers. Frederika had slipped the paper into Alix’s palm in the damp corridor of the Zeeland dock, seconds before the Penose boatmen ushered the team onto the cargo transport for Amsterdam. There had been no commcode on the linen, no Matrix routing tag for corporate spiders to trace, only an address by the sea, an hour after midnight, written in a steady, slanted fountain-pen script.

With the arrest warrants scrubbed from the Maas-Waker grids and the royal compensation sitting untouched on a certified stick, operational tradecraft dictated taking the midnight night-train and burning the skin he had worn before dawn. Yet Alix had remained. Across the chest of the heavy charcoal overcoat, double-faced Belgian wool bought from an old tailor on the Maas quay, a three-inch furrow over the sternum had been carefully darned with dark thread. Beneath the mended wool and the silk lining, a deep, yellowed bruise still ached against the breastbone where an HTR rifle slug had slammed into Alix’s frame on the Escher Palace mezzanine. The flexible skinshield underlayer and fragile bone had absorbed the kinetic force before the lead could reach the woman standing behind him.

The glass door at the landward end of the pavilion clicked open, letting in a brief gust of salt air and the distant hush of the surf. Lady Frederika van de Poll stepped inside, shaking the ocean mist from a simple coat of heather wool and unwinding a knitted scarf from her throat. Without the towering mirrors and gilded ceilings of the palace atrium, she looked smaller, a woman in her late thirties carrying the ingrained exhaustion of a life spent standing two steps behind a throne. Her eyes found Alix in the amber glow of the table lamp. For a heartbeat, she paused by the threshold, her hand resting on the brass latch as if confirming that the figure by the window was not a trick of the moonlight. Then she walked across the pine planks and slipped into the chair opposite him.

"You came," she said softly, her English carrying the clipped, gentle cadence of the Dutch court, pitched low enough to blend into the wash of the waves beneath the timbers.

"You gave me the time," Alix replied. The voice was the calm, androgynous Mid-Atlantic register Velvet had shaped for the palace ingress, smooth and unhurried, though Velvet consciously held the resonance at a whisper, refusing to let its acoustic weight press against her across the small table. He lifted the teapot and poured warm, golden chamomile into her cup.

Frederika wrapped both hands around the porcelain, looking down into the steam as the water swelled and sighed against the pilings below. "The news feeds in The Hague have already moved on to the trade tariffs," she murmured. "The official ministry bulletin states that a team of freelance security contractors assisted palace staff during an isolated criminal breach. Freelance mercenaries, they called you." She looked up, her gaze studying the refined bridge of Alix’s nose, the quiet symmetry of the cheekbones, and the disciplined stillness of the shoulders resting against the dark wood. "My family has served the House of Oranje-Nassau for two hundred years, Alix. Untitled nobility. We do not sit in the portraits; we are the ones who hold the doors, arrange the schedules, and pour the tea before the ministers arrive. When you spend twenty years standing quietly against the tapestries of royal salons and megacorporate boardrooms, you learn to recognize the things that money cannot ordinarily buy."

A wave rolled beneath the floorboards, rocking the reflection of the candle flame in the dark glass. Frederika tilted her head slightly, studying Alix with an intensity that seemed almost clinical. "You are not a street mercenary from Europort. I have seen people like you twice in my life: once standing beside a Saeder-Krupp director in Essen, and once in the retinue of an Imperial envoy from Neo-Tokyo. Bespoke graces. People sculpted by master artisans and corporate fortunes to command the air in a room. Creatures like you are built for kings and chairwomen. Not for the help."

Velvet kept Alix’s hands flat and open on the white linen, feeling the rhythmic thrum of the sea through the soles of his boots. He did not blink, letting her look.

"On the third floor, when I opened Mister Brooks’s door and smelled the blood, my legs turned to stone," she continued, a faint tremor touching the corner of her mouth. Her hands tightened around the warm porcelain, the index finger showing the faint, circular seam of a replaced fingertip. "I knew the strike teams were coming through the roof. I knew I had to reach the lift. Decisions were screaming in my head, and my body simply locked. Then you looked at me. You spoke my name, and the terror went out of my blood like a lamp being blown out. And ten seconds later, when the rifle flashed from the gallery, you stepped directly into the line of fire."

Her gaze searched Alix’s face, carrying a vulnerability so raw it made the old cartilage scars behind Velvet’s ears ache. In the amber lamplight, her eyes held an unblinking, crystalline stillness. They were deltaware throwbacks, optical prosthetics crafted with exquisite perfection to look entirely human, yet entirely decoupled from the wireless Matrix. They were the un-hackable lenses that had recorded the blood on Brooks’s floor, the traitor guards, and the green diagnostic flash that had cleared the team’s name before Princess Juliana-Marie.

"I have not slept since Zeeland," she confessed, her voice dropping until it was barely louder than the tide. "Every time I close my eyes, I feel your hand on my arm and hear your voice in the stairwell. I feel this overwhelming, aching pull toward you, Alix. And because I know what creatures like you are designed to do in those boardrooms, I am terrified. My eyes hold the objective optical record on an isolated chip, proving you took a bullet meant for my chest. Yet my heart cannot tell if what I feel is real gratitude, or if I am just a foolish court clerk drowning in the spells engrained into your skin."

The silence in the pavilion settled deep into the hollow center of Velvet's chest, striking the exact wound he had carried from Manila to Singapore, from the rain-soaked noodle carts of Seattle to the canals of Amsterdam. She was asking the question he asked his own reflection every time the masks came off in the dark: whether anything born from a corporate lie could ever be touched without poisoning the person who held it.

Velvet drew a slow breath into the lower diaphragm, letting the last trace of biological gravity drain out of the vessel. The subtle, protective posture of the corporate auditor softened, leaving the air between them still as winter water, stripped of all harmonic persuasion. He reached up with ungloved fingers and undid the top two buttons of the heavy charcoal coat, parting the lapel to reveal the stitched, darkened seam over the sternum where the ballistic weave had caught the lead.

"Look at the coat, Frederika," Alix said gently.

Her gaze dropped to the mended tear over his heart, her breath catching softly.

"You are right about what made me," Alix said, the quiet words moving in time with the surf beneath the floor. "I was built in a place where every smile, every syllable, and every angle of the bone was measured to make a room compliant. Part of my existence is to be a thousand things to a thousand people, that which shifts its shape so the world sees whatever it wishes to see. A voice can trick the blood into slowing down when glass shatters. Symmetry can make a stranger trust you in the dark. The world out there is full of illusions trying to change what you feel. You are wise to doubt the voice."

Alix reached across the small table, laying one open palm upward on the white linen beside her saucer, leaving the distance between them entirely hers to cross.

"An illusion cannot catch lead."

Frederika’s eyes lifted from the mended bullet scar to the dark depth of Alix’s gaze. Slowly, hesitantly, she uncurled her fingers from the teacup and laid her hand into Alix’s open palm. Her skin was warm, slightly calloused at the knuckle, the cool, flush rim of the datajack on her index finger resting directly against his life-line.

"When I walk off this pier tonight, I will have to become other names and other faces to survive the people who built me," Alix murmured, his fingers closing gently around hers. "That is the only way I know how to stay free. Yet I can give you one truth that no boardroom ever paid for. I will never wear this face for another soul. No king, no executive, and no other room will ever hear this cadence again. For as long as I live, these hands, these eyes, and this voice exist only for you."

The guarded tension around Frederika’s temples eased. A single tear escaped the corner of her right eye, slipping down her cheek with the quiet relief of a woman who had finally been seen in a room where she was meant to be invisible. She turned her palm against his, holding fast to his knuckles.

They did not speak of the palace, the Princess, or the corporate shadow war gathering across the European borders. As the chamomile cooled in the porcelain cups, they sat together by the glass, listening to the North Sea roll onto the sand in its endless measure, wave upon wave washing the shore clean.

When the coastal tram bell chimed at the head of the pier to mark the last run toward the city center, Frederika stood and drew her heather coat around her shoulders. She looked down at Alix one last time, pressing his hand between both of hers before stepping out into the salt mist.

Velvet remained at the table by the window until her footsteps faded down the boardwalk. In the dark glass, the reflection of Alix Lindqvist sat motionless in the radiator warmth, honoring the promise. Only after the distant hum of the tram died away into the dunes did Velvet close his eyes, allowing the bone-latches to release and folding Alix away into the silent vault of his memory, untouched, unsoiled, and kept solely for her.
